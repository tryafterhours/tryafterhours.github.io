How to ask out a coworker without making it awkward

You've noticed them for weeks, maybe months. You'd notice if they weren't in on Monday. But you also have to see them at work tomorrow, whatever they say.

Here's how to ask out a colleague in a way that's respectful, low-pressure and easy for both of you.

First, check three things

  1. They're not in your reporting line. If either of you manages the other, directly or indirectly, don't ask. It puts them in an unfair position, and most company policies forbid it.
  2. You've actually talked. A few real conversations, not just nods at the coffee machine, make asking feel natural instead of out of nowhere.
  3. You can handle a no. If a no would make you act differently at work, wait until it wouldn't.

Read the signals (gently)

Signs they might be interested:

  • They start conversations with you, not just reply.
  • They remember small things you mentioned.
  • They find reasons to stop by, or join the same lunch or chai break.

None of these are proof. People can just be friendly. Treat signals as a reason to ask lightly, not as a guarantee.

Ask small, not big

The easiest ask is something casual, outside work, with an easy way out:

"I'm trying that new café near the metro on Saturday. Want to come?"

Why this works:

  • It's specific, so it's easy to say yes.
  • It's low-stakes, so it's easy to say no.
  • It's outside work, so it feels like a date, not a meeting.

Avoid asking in front of others, over work chat, or at a team event after a drink or two.

If they say no

Say "no worries at all" and mean it. Then:

  • Don't ask again.
  • Don't change how you treat them at work.
  • Don't discuss it with colleagues.

Handled well, a no is forgotten within a week. Handled badly, it becomes the thing everyone knows about.

If they say yes

  • Keep it private until you both want to share.
  • Keep work as work: no favouritism, and no flirting in work chat.
  • Check your company's policy on workplace relationships.
